# Solutions to Meteorology Exam Study Guide: Chapters 6–11
## Practice Problems

*Try these on your own first, then check your work with the answer key below.*

1. The surface temperature is 30 °C and the dew point is 14 °C. At what altitude will the base of a cumulus cloud form?
2. A radiosonde measures an environmental lapse rate of 7 °C/km. Is this layer absolutely stable, absolutely unstable, or conditionally unstable? Explain.
3. A parcel at sea level (T = 25 °C, Td = 17 °C) is forced over a mountain 2500 m high. Find the cloud base, the temperature at the summit, and the temperature and dew point when it returns to sea level on the leeward side. (Use 6 °C/km for the moist rate.)
4. A weather station at 800 m elevation reads a station pressure of 915 mb. What is its approximate sea-level pressure?
5. City A reports 1016 mb and City B, 300 km away, reports 1004 mb. What is the pressure gradient in mb per 100 km? Which way would the PGF point?
6. Draw (or describe) the vertical temperature profiles that produce snow, sleet, and freezing rain at the ground.
7. You are in the Northern Hemisphere standing with your back to the surface wind. In which direction is the center of low pressure?
8. Why is the moist adiabatic lapse rate smaller than the dry adiabatic lapse rate?
9. Explain why deserts are commonly found near 30° latitude.
10. Describe the changes in temperature, wind direction, pressure, and sky conditions you would observe as a cold front passes your location.
11. What air mass would you expect to bring a hot, humid, stormy summer day to the Midwest, and where does it come from?
12. During an El Niño, what happens to the trade winds and to upwelling off the coast of Peru?
13. Explain the difference between a cold-type and a warm-type occlusion.
14. Why do surface winds cross the isobars, while winds aloft blow parallel to them?

---

## Answer Key

1. H = 125 × (30 − 14) = 125 × 16 = **2000 m**.
2. **Conditionally unstable**, because 7 °C/km falls between the MALR (~6 °C/km) and the DALR (10 °C/km). An unsaturated parcel would be stable, but a saturated parcel would be unstable.
3. Cloud base = 125 × (25 − 17) = **1000 m**. At 1000 m, T = 25 − 10 = 15 °C and Td = 17 − 2 = 15 °C (saturated). From 1000 to 2500 m (1.5 km × 6 °C/km = 9 °C): summit T = Td = **6 °C**. Descending 2500 m on the leeward side: T = 6 + 25 = **31 °C**; Td = 6 + (2.5 × 2) = **11 °C**. The air is warmer and drier on the lee side.
4. 915 + (800 ÷ 100 × 10) = 915 + 80 = **≈ 995 mb**.
5. 12 mb ÷ 300 km = **4 mb per 100 km**. The PGF points **from City A toward City B** (from high to low pressure), perpendicular to the isobars.
6. **Snow:** below freezing all the way from cloud to ground. **Sleet:** cold layer aloft, then a warm (above 0 °C) layer where the snow melts, then a **deep** below-freezing layer near the surface where the drops refreeze into pellets. **Freezing rain:** same as sleet but the surface cold layer is **shallow**, so drops stay liquid (supercooled) and freeze on contact.
7. Turn clockwise about 30°; the low is then to your **left** (to your left and slightly ahead of where you were originally facing).
8. When a saturated parcel rises, water vapor condenses and releases **latent heat**, which partly offsets the cooling caused by expansion.
9. Air in the Hadley cell that rose at the equator **sinks near 30°** in the subtropical highs. Sinking air **warms by compression** and its relative humidity drops, which suppresses clouds and precipitation.
10. Before: warm, humid, south/southwest winds, falling pressure. During: showers or thunderstorms, gusty winds. After: **cooler and drier air**, winds shift to the **west/northwest**, **pressure rises**, and skies **clear**.
11. **Maritime tropical (mT)** air from the **Gulf of Mexico**.
12. The trade winds **weaken** (or reverse), and **upwelling weakens**, so the water off Peru becomes **warmer** than normal.
13. In a **cold-type occlusion**, the air behind the cold front is colder than the air ahead of the warm front, so it wedges **under** it. In a **warm-type occlusion**, the air behind the cold front is milder, so the cold front rides **up and over** the colder air ahead of the warm front.
14. Near the surface, **friction** slows the wind, which weakens the Coriolis force; the PGF is then stronger, so the wind turns **across the isobars toward low pressure**. Aloft, there is little friction, so the PGF and Coriolis force **balance** and the wind blows **parallel** to the isobars (geostrophic wind).
