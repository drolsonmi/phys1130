## Chapter 12 – Mid-Latitude Cyclones

A **mid-latitude cyclone** (also called an **extratropical cyclone** or a **wave cyclone**) is a large low-pressure system, often 1000–2500 km across, that forms along the polar front in the middle latitudes. These storms bring most of the day-to-day weather changes in places like the United States, and they last several days to about a week.

### 12.1 Polar Front Theory (the Norwegian Cyclone Model)

- Developed by **Vilhelm and Jacob Bjerknes** and colleagues at the **Bergen School** in Norway around the time of World War I. The idea of a battle line between air masses is where the term "**front**" comes from.
- Mid-latitude cyclones form along the **polar front**, the boundary between **cold polar air** to the north and **warm tropical air** to the south (in the NH).
- The model describes the storm's **life cycle** as a sequence of stages (below), with **warm, cold, and occluded fronts** organized around a central low.
- The polar front sits beneath the **polar front jet stream**, which is why the two are so closely tied together.

### 12.2 Stages of a Mid-Latitude Cyclone

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Stationary front** | Cold air and warm air sit side by side along the polar front, with winds blowing parallel to the front in opposite directions (wind shear across the front). |
| **2. Frontal wave (incipient cyclone)** | A disturbance, often from upper-level support, puts a **kink (wave)** in the front. Cold air starts pushing south and warm air pushes north. Pressure begins to fall at the tip of the wave. |
| **3. Open wave** | A **distinct low** forms at the wave's crest, with a **warm front** extending east and a **cold front** extending south/southwest. The region between them is the **warm sector**. Winds circulate counterclockwise (NH), and precipitation forms ahead of the warm front and along the cold front. |
| **4. Mature stage (beginning of occlusion)** | The faster **cold front catches up to the warm front**, forming an **occluded front** near the low center. The storm is usually at its **strongest** (lowest central pressure) around this time. |
| **5. Occluded stage** | The occlusion lengthens; the warm air is lifted off the ground. The low is cut off from its supply of warm, moist air. |
| **6. Dissipating stage** | With no more warm, moist air flowing in and cold air surrounding the low, the storm **weakens and fills** (central pressure rises). |

> The point where the cold, warm, and occluded fronts meet is called the **triple point**, a common spot for new storm development and severe weather.

**Typical weather around a mature NH cyclone:**
- **North and northeast of the low** (ahead of the warm front): widespread clouds and steady precipitation; snow or freezing rain in winter on the cold side.
- **Warm sector** (south of the low, between the fronts): warm and humid, south winds, possible thunderstorms.
- **Along the cold front:** showers and thunderstorms, possibly a squall line.
- **Behind the cold front** (west of the low): colder, drier air, northwest winds, clearing; possible wraparound snow near the low.

### 12.3 Cyclogenesis

- **Cyclogenesis** = the **formation or strengthening** of a cyclone (a low-pressure system). It is measured by a **drop in central pressure**.
- **Common cyclogenesis regions in North America:**
  - **East (lee) side of the Rocky Mountains** — **lee-side lows** such as the **Colorado low** and the **Alberta clipper** (fast-moving, usually light snow).
  - **Gulf of Mexico** — Gulf lows that tap deep moisture.
  - **East Coast** (near Cape Hatteras) — **nor'easters**, which strengthen over the warm Gulf Stream and bring heavy snow, rain, wind, and coastal flooding to the Northeast.
- **Lee-side cyclogenesis:** As air descends the east side of a mountain range, the air column **stretches** vertically, which spins it up (increases cyclonic rotation) and helps a low form.
- **Bombogenesis ("bomb cyclone"):** **Explosive** cyclogenesis, when central pressure drops at least **24 mb in 24 hours** (the threshold is adjusted for latitude). Most common over warm ocean currents in winter.
- Cyclones need **horizontal temperature contrast** (a strong temperature gradient) to grow; they get energy by moving warm air poleward and upward and cold air equatorward and downward.

### 12.4 Upper-Air Support

A surface low cannot develop or strengthen on its own. It needs help from the **upper atmosphere**.

#### Convergence and Divergence

- **Convergence:** air piling up (flowing into a region). **Divergence:** air spreading out (flowing out of a region).
- At a surface low, air **converges** and **rises**. If nothing removes that air aloft, it piles up, pressure rises, and the low **fills** (weakens).
- **Key rule:** A surface low **strengthens** when **upper-level divergence is greater than surface convergence**. More air leaves the top of the column than enters at the bottom, so surface pressure **falls**.
- A surface low **weakens** when **surface convergence is greater than upper-level divergence**.
- The reverse holds for surface highs: **convergence aloft** feeds **sinking air** and **divergence at the surface**.

#### Where Is the Divergence Aloft?

- On upper-air charts, the westerlies flow in a pattern of **ridges** and **troughs**.
- **Divergence aloft** is found **downstream (east) of an upper-level trough**, between the trough and the next ridge. This is the favorable spot for a **surface low** to develop.
- **Convergence aloft** is found **upstream (west) of the trough**, between the ridge and the trough, which supports a **surface high**.
- **Shortwave troughs** (smaller ripples moving through the larger long-wave pattern) are often the trigger that starts cyclogenesis.
- In a developing storm, the upper-level trough sits **west of the surface low**, so the system **tilts westward with height**. When the upper low sits directly over the surface low (vertically stacked), the storm is mature and will soon weaken.
- **Temperature advection** helps too: **warm air advection** east of the low builds the downstream ridge, while **cold air advection** west of the low deepens the trough, which strengthens the whole system.

#### The Jet Stream

- The **polar front jet stream** **steers** mid-latitude cyclones; surface lows tend to move in the general direction of the upper-level flow.
- **Jet streaks** are regions of **fastest winds** within the jet stream. As air speeds up entering a jet streak and slows down leaving it, regions of convergence and divergence form.
- For a straight jet streak in the NH, the **divergence** (and rising air) is found in the:
  - **Left exit region** (the poleward side, downstream end)
  - **Right entrance region** (the equatorward side, upstream end)
- A surface low positioned beneath one of these divergent quadrants (especially the **left exit**) tends to **intensify rapidly**.
- **Seasonal pattern:** The jet is **stronger and farther south in winter** (larger temperature contrast), so mid-latitude cyclones are **stronger and more frequent in winter** across the U.S.

## Chapter 13 – Weather Forecasting

### 13.1 Why Do We Forecast?

- **Protect life and property:** warnings for tornadoes, hurricanes, floods, blizzards, heat waves.
- **Support the economy:** agriculture (planting, harvesting, frost protection), aviation (routing, turbulence, icing), shipping, energy (predicting heating and cooling demand), construction, transportation, and emergency management.
- **Everyday planning:** what to wear, travel decisions, outdoor events.
- The National Weather Service's mission is to provide forecasts and warnings to protect life and property and enhance the national economy, and it works toward a **Weather-Ready Nation**.

### 13.2 Forecasting Tools

**Observations (data collection):**
- **Surface stations** — automated systems such as **ASOS** at airports measure temperature, dew point, pressure, wind, visibility, and precipitation.
- **Radiosondes** — weather balloons launched **twice a day** (00Z and 12Z) at stations worldwide to measure temperature, humidity, pressure, and wind through the atmosphere.
- **Weather satellites** — **geostationary** satellites (such as **GOES**) stay over one spot and give continuous images; **polar-orbiting** satellites pass over the whole Earth at lower altitude with higher detail. Visible, infrared, and water vapor imagery.
- **Doppler radar** (NEXRAD) — precipitation intensity, motion, rotation, and precipitation type.
- **Aircraft reports**, **ships and buoys**, **wind profilers**, and lightning detection networks.

**Analysis and prediction tools:**
- **Surface and upper-air maps** (analyzed for fronts, highs, lows, troughs, ridges).
- **Soundings / Skew-T diagrams** for stability (Ch. 6).
- **Meteograms** — charts showing how forecast variables change over time at one location.
- **Numerical weather prediction (NWP) models** — supercomputers solve the physics equations of the atmosphere (motion, energy, moisture) on a 3-D grid, stepping forward in time. Examples: **GFS** (American global model), **ECMWF** (European global model), **NAM**, and **HRRR** (high-resolution, short-range).
- **AWIPS** — the computer workstation system NWS forecasters use to display and combine all of these data.
- The **human forecaster** — interprets the models, knows local effects (mountains, lakes, coasts), and communicates the forecast.

**Limits of forecasting:**
- Models start from **imperfect observations** (gaps over oceans, mountains, and sparsely populated areas) and use **simplified physics** and finite grid spacing.
- The atmosphere is **chaotic**: tiny errors in the starting conditions grow over time (the "**butterfly effect**," described by Edward Lorenz). This limits useful detailed forecasts to about **two weeks** at most.

### 13.3 Forecast Ranges

| Range | Time period | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Nowcast (very short range)** | 0 to about **6 hours** | Based heavily on current observations, radar, and satellite; extrapolating what is happening now. Used for thunderstorms, warnings. |
| **Short-range** | About **6 hours to 2½–3 days** | Models are quite accurate; the most detailed public forecasts. |
| **Medium-range** | About **3 to 8½ days** | Models still useful, but accuracy drops; ensembles important. |
| **Long-range** | Beyond about **8½ days** (weeks, months, seasons) | Cannot predict day-to-day weather; gives **outlooks** of whether conditions will be above or below normal (e.g., NOAA **Climate Prediction Center** 6–10 day, 8–14 day, monthly, and seasonal outlooks). Relies on patterns like **ENSO** (Ch. 10). |

*Note: Exact cutoffs vary a little between textbooks and agencies; know the general order and what each range is good for.*

**Forecast accuracy decreases as the range increases.** A forecast is considered to have **skill** if it beats a simple baseline such as **persistence** or **climatology**.

### 13.4 Types (Methods) of Forecasting

| Method | How it works | When it works well / poorly |
|---|---|---|
| **Persistence** | Assumes **future weather will be the same as now** ("tomorrow will be like today"). | Good for very short periods and in steady patterns (e.g., a stagnant summer high, or the dry season). Fails when weather systems change. |
| **Trend (steady-state)** | Assumes weather systems will keep moving at the **same speed and direction** (extrapolating a storm's path). | Good for a few hours (nowcasting a line of storms). Fails when systems speed up, slow down, turn, strengthen, or weaken. |
| **Weather-type (pattern recognition)** | Classifies the large-scale pattern into a familiar **type** that tends to produce certain weather (e.g., "a trough over the West usually means a storm here in two days"). | Useful when a recognizable pattern is in place; depends on forecaster experience. |
| **Analogue** | Searches past records for a situation that **closely matches** today's (an analog), then forecasts that the weather will evolve the same way it did then. | Works if a good match exists; the atmosphere rarely repeats exactly, so it is limited. |
| **Climatology** | Uses **long-term averages and statistics** for that date and place (e.g., "it usually rains 3 days per week in May"). | Best baseline for **long ranges** (beyond ~10 days); poor for specific day-to-day forecasts. |
| **Statistical / Numerical (models)** | **Numerical** models solve atmospheric equations; **statistical** methods relate model output to past observations to correct biases (**MOS – Model Output Statistics**). | The backbone of modern forecasting, especially short- and medium-range. Still limited by chaos and imperfect data. |

#### Probability Forecasts

- A **Probability of Precipitation (PoP)** describes the chance that **any given point** in the forecast area receives **at least 0.01 inch** of precipitation during the forecast period.
- **PoP = C × A**, where **C** = forecaster's confidence that precipitation will occur somewhere in the area, and **A** = the fraction of the area expected to receive it.
  - *Example:* 100% confident that rain will fall over 40% of the area → PoP = 1.0 × 0.40 = **40%**.
  - *Example:* 80% confident that rain will cover 50% of the area → PoP = 0.8 × 0.5 = **40%**.

### 13.5 Advisories, Watches, and Warnings

| Product | Meaning | What you should do |
|---|---|---|
| **Advisory** | A hazardous event is occurring or expected that is **less serious** than a warning but could cause **inconvenience** or be dangerous if you are careless (e.g., Winter Weather Advisory, Wind Advisory, Dense Fog Advisory, Heat Advisory). | **Be aware**; use caution. |
| **Watch** | Conditions are **favorable** for a hazardous event to develop; it is **possible**, but location and timing are uncertain (e.g., Tornado Watch, Hurricane Watch). | **Be prepared**: review your plan, monitor the weather, know where you'd shelter. |
| **Warning** | The hazardous event is **occurring, imminent, or highly likely** and threatens life and property (e.g., Tornado Warning, Severe Thunderstorm Warning, Hurricane Warning). | **Take action now** to protect yourself. |

**Who issues them:**
- **Tornado and Severe Thunderstorm Watches** are issued by the NWS **Storm Prediction Center (SPC)** in Norman, Oklahoma, and typically cover large areas (several counties or states) for several hours.
- **Warnings** are issued by local **Weather Forecast Offices (WFOs)** for specific, smaller areas (storm-based **polygons**), based on radar or spotter reports.
- **Hurricane Watches and Warnings** come from the **National Hurricane Center (NHC)**.

**Severe thunderstorm criteria (NWS):** hail **1 inch** in diameter or larger, wind gusts of **58 mph** or greater, and/or a tornado.

> **Upcoming change:** The NWS has decided to phase out the "**Advisory**" headline (and "Special Weather Statement") and replace it with **plain-language headlines** that name the hazard directly, because research showed many people confuse "Advisory" with "Watch." The underlying information will still be issued. Watches and warnings will remain. Check weather.gov for the current status of this change.

### 13.6 Ensemble Forecasting

- Because the atmosphere is chaotic and observations are imperfect, a single model run (a **deterministic** forecast) can be misleading.
- An **ensemble forecast** runs a model **many times** (often 20–50 **members**), each starting from **slightly different initial conditions** (and sometimes slightly different model physics or different models altogether).
- **How to interpret it:**
  - If the members **agree** (small **spread**), forecasters have **high confidence**.
  - If the members **disagree** (large spread), confidence is **low**, and the forecast should express uncertainty.
  - The **ensemble mean** (average of all members) is often more accurate than any single run, especially in the medium range.
  - Ensembles allow **probability forecasts** (e.g., "70% of members show more than 6 inches of snow").
- **Spaghetti plots** draw one contour line (for example, a 500 mb height line, or hurricane tracks) from each member; tightly bundled "spaghetti" = high confidence, widely scattered = low confidence.
- Examples: NOAA's **GEFS** (Global Ensemble Forecast System) and the **ECMWF Ensemble**.

## Chapter 14 – Thunderstorms and Lightning

A **thunderstorm** is a storm containing **lightning and thunder**, produced by a **cumulonimbus** cloud.

**Ingredients for a thunderstorm:**
1. **Moisture** (in the lower atmosphere)
2. **Instability** (warm, moist air at low levels that will keep rising once lifted; see Ch. 6)
3. **Lift** (a trigger: fronts, dry lines, outflow boundaries, sea breezes, mountains, surface heating)
4. For **organized, long-lived, severe** storms: **vertical wind shear** (change in wind speed and/or direction with height)

**The amount of wind shear largely decides the storm type:** weak shear → ordinary cells; moderate shear → multicells; strong shear → supercells.

### 14.1 Ordinary Cell Storms (Single-Cell or Air-Mass Thunderstorms)

- Form in **warm, humid air with weak vertical wind shear**, often away from fronts, typically on summer afternoons from surface heating.
- Usually **short-lived** (about **30 minutes to 1 hour**) and **rarely severe**, though they can produce brief heavy rain, small hail, lightning, and gusty winds (sometimes a microburst).
- **Three stages:**
  1. **Cumulus (developing) stage:** **Updrafts only.** Cumulus grows into towering cumulus as rising air condenses and releases latent heat. Little or no precipitation reaches the ground yet.
  2. **Mature stage:** **Both updrafts and downdrafts.** Precipitation falls and drags air down; evaporation cools the air, strengthening the **downdraft**. The storm is at its **most intense**: heaviest rain, lightning, possible hail, gusty winds. An **anvil** spreads at the top of the troposphere (tropopause). An **overshooting top** may poke above the anvil.
  3. **Dissipating stage:** **Downdrafts dominate.** Because there is little wind shear, the downdraft falls into the updraft and **cuts off the storm's supply** of warm, moist air. Light rain, then the cloud evaporates, often leaving an anvil remnant.
- **Key idea:** Without shear, the storm **destroys itself** because its downdraft chokes its updraft.

### 14.2 Multicell Storms

**Multicell storms** are clusters or lines of storms in which **new cells** continually form as **older cells** die. Each cell has the ordinary life cycle, but the system can last for **hours**.

#### Wind Shear
- **Moderate vertical wind shear** tilts the storm so the **downdraft and precipitation fall away from the updraft** instead of into it.
- This separation allows the storm to persist and new cells to keep forming.

#### Gust Front / Outflow Boundaries
- Cold, dense downdraft air spreads out along the ground beneath and ahead of the storm. Its leading edge is the **gust front**.
- When the gust front passes: sudden **wind shift**, strong **gusts**, a sharp **temperature drop**, and a **pressure rise** (a small "**mesohigh**").
- The gust front acts like a **mini cold front**, **lifting** warm, moist air to trigger **new cells** (this is how a multicell storm regenerates).
- Clouds that may form along it: a **shelf cloud** (attached, wedge-shaped, on the leading edge) and a **roll cloud** (detached, tube-shaped).
- An **outflow boundary** is a gust front that persists after the storm dies; it can be seen on radar as a thin line and can **trigger new storms hours later** (especially where two boundaries collide).

#### Squall Lines
- A **line of thunderstorms**, often hundreds of km long, with the storms side by side.
- **Prefrontal squall lines** form in the warm sector **ahead of a cold front** (often 100–300 km ahead), and squall lines can also form along a dry line or a cold front itself.
- Main hazards: **damaging straight-line winds**, heavy rain, hail, and brief tornadoes.
- On radar, part of the line may bulge forward into a **bow echo**, a sign of a **rear-inflow jet** of strong wind descending behind the line and producing severe straight-line winds at the bow's apex.

#### Derechos
- A **derecho** (Spanish for "straight") is a **widespread, long-lived windstorm** associated with a fast-moving band of thunderstorms (often a series of **bow echoes**).
- NWS/SPC definition: the wind damage swath extends **more than about 240 miles (≈400 km)**, with wind gusts of at least **58 mph** along most of its length. Winds can reach **100+ mph** (hurricane force).
- Derecho-producing systems often move **50 mph or faster**.
- Types include **progressive** (a single bow echo moving along a nearly stationary front, mainly warm season) and **serial** (multiple bow echoes along an extensive squall line, often with a strong low-pressure system).
- Most common in the **Midwest and Great Plains** in **late spring and summer**.

#### Mesoscale Convective Complex (MCC)
- A large, **nearly circular** cluster of thunderstorms covering an area often larger than **100,000 km²** (about the size of a state like Iowa), identified by its huge, cold cloud shield on **infrared satellite** images.
- Lasts **at least 6 hours**, often **12+ hours**.
- Most common over the **Great Plains in summer**, typically **forming in the evening and peaking overnight**, often fed by the nocturnal **low-level jet**.
- Benefits and hazards: provides much of the **summer rainfall** for crops in the central U.S., but can cause **flash flooding**, hail, and damaging winds.
- MCCs, squall lines, and similar large organized systems all belong to the broader group of **mesoscale convective systems (MCSs)**.

#### Microbursts
- A **microburst** is a small, intense **downburst** affecting an area **less than 4 km (2.5 mi)** across, usually lasting **less than 15 minutes**.
- Winds can exceed **100 mph**, causing damage similar to a weak tornado, but the damage is **straight-line** and spreads **outward** from the point of impact (a "**starburst**" pattern), unlike tornado damage, which converges and swirls.
- **Wet microbursts** come with heavy rain; **dry microbursts** occur beneath high-based storms in dry climates (like the western U.S.), where only **virga** may be visible, because rain evaporates and chills the air.
- **Aviation hazard:** a plane flying through a microburst first gets a **headwind** (extra lift), then a **downdraft**, then a sudden **tailwind** (loss of lift) close to the ground. Microbursts caused several airline crashes before airports added detection systems (**Doppler radar** and wind sensors).

### 14.3 Supercell Storms

A **supercell** is a severe thunderstorm with a **single, intense, rotating updraft**. Supercells are the **least common** storm type but the **most dangerous**, producing most **large hail**, **damaging winds**, and **strong to violent tornadoes**. They can last **several hours**.

#### Single Rotating Updraft (the Mesocyclone)
- The rotating updraft is called a **mesocyclone**, typically **3–10 km** wide.

**Wind shear (how rotation begins):**
- **Strong vertical wind shear**, where winds **increase in speed** and **change direction (veer, turning clockwise)** with height, creates **horizontal spin** in the lower atmosphere, like a pencil rolling between your hands.
- A strong **updraft tilts** that horizontal spinning tube **into the vertical**, producing a rotating updraft.
- Because shear keeps the updraft and downdraft **separated**, the storm can maintain itself for hours.

**Instability:**
- Supercells need **high instability** (large **CAPE**) to produce updrafts strong enough to tilt and stretch the rotation and to hold up large hailstones. Updrafts can exceed **100 mph**.
- Often there is a **cap** (a warm, stable layer aloft, or inversion) that holds back storms early in the day, letting heat and moisture build until a storm finally breaks through explosively.

**Other supercell features:**
- **Overshooting top** above the anvil (sign of a very strong updraft).
- **Forward-flank downdraft (FFD)** with heavy rain and hail, and **rear-flank downdraft (RFD)**, which wraps around the mesocyclone and is important for tornado formation.
- **Flanking line** of cumulus towers feeding into the main updraft.
- **Bounded weak echo region (BWER)** on radar: a precipitation-free "vault" where the updraft is so strong that rain can't fall.
- Supercells are sometimes classified as **classic**, **low-precipitation (LP)**, and **high-precipitation (HP)** types.

#### Wall Clouds
- A **wall cloud** is an isolated, often **rotating lowering** of the cloud base beneath the **rain-free base** of the main updraft, usually on the storm's **rear (southwest) side**.
- Forms where moist, rain-cooled air is pulled into the updraft and condenses at a lower level.
- A **persistent, rotating** wall cloud with rapid upward motion often appears **10–20 minutes or more before** a tornado forms; not every wall cloud produces a tornado.
- Don't confuse it with a **shelf cloud**, which is on the **leading edge** of the outflow and does not rotate.

### 14.4 Lightning

**Lightning** is a giant **electrical discharge** (spark) between regions of opposite charge in the atmosphere. It can occur within a cloud, between clouds, from cloud to air, or from cloud to ground. **Thunder** is the sound produced when the air along the lightning channel is heated almost instantly to about **30,000 °C (~54,000 °F)**, roughly five times hotter than the sun's surface, and expands explosively.

#### Caused by Static Electricity; the Strike Closes a Circuit
- **Charge separation:** Inside a cumulonimbus, collisions between **graupel (soft hail)** and **small ice crystals** in the presence of **supercooled water** transfer charge. Lighter, positively charged ice crystals are carried **up** by the updraft; heavier, negatively charged graupel stays **lower**.
- **Typical charge structure:** **positive** charge in the **upper** cloud and anvil; **negative** charge in the **middle/lower** cloud; often a small **positive** pocket near the base.
- The negative cloud base **induces a positive charge** on the ground beneath it, and that region **follows the storm** like a shadow.
- Air is normally a good **insulator**. When the charge difference becomes large enough to break down the air's resistance, a discharge occurs. The lightning channel **connects the oppositely charged regions**, completing an **electrical circuit** and neutralizing the charge for a moment, much like the static shock you get after shuffling across carpet, but billions of times stronger.

#### Stepped Leaders
- A cloud-to-ground strike begins with a faint, **negatively charged** channel of ionized air called a **stepped leader**, which moves down from the cloud in **steps** of about **50 meters**, pausing briefly between steps, often **branching** as it goes.
- It is **nearly invisible** to the eye and takes only a fraction of a second to approach the ground.
- As it nears the ground, **positively charged streamers** (upward leaders) rise from tall objects, such as trees, poles, buildings, and even people.
- When a streamer **connects** with the stepped leader (usually within ~30–100 m of the ground), the circuit is complete.

#### Return Stroke
- Immediately after connection, a powerful surge of current, the **return stroke**, races **upward** from the ground to the cloud along the channel the leader created.
- The return stroke is the **bright flash** we see; it carries most of the current (tens of thousands of amperes) and produces the intense heating that makes **thunder**.
- Typically, **dart leaders** travel back down the same channel and trigger additional return strokes, so most flashes contain **3–4 strokes**, giving lightning its **flickering** appearance.
- **Positive lightning** comes from the positively charged anvil, can strike **many miles away** from the storm ("**bolt from the blue**"), and carries a stronger, longer-lasting current. It is less common but more dangerous, and it causes many wildfires.

#### Thunder and Distance
- Light arrives almost instantly
- Lightning is a current of electricity that rapidly heats up the air
    - As air is heated, it rapidly expands, creating a pressure wave that hits our ears
    - Sound travels about **1 mile in 5 seconds** (about **1 km in 3 seconds**).
        - **Distance (miles) ≈ seconds between flash and thunder ÷ 5**
- Thunder is rarely heard more than about **10 miles** away. If you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck.

#### Different Types of Lightning

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Forked lightning** | Cloud-to-ground lightning showing a bright main channel with **branches**. |
| **Ribbon lightning** | Strong **winds blow the channel sideways** between successive return strokes, so the strokes appear **side by side** like a ribbon. |
| **Bead lightning** | The fading channel appears to **break into a string of glowing segments** (beads) rather than a continuous line. |
| **Ball lightning** | A rare, poorly understood **glowing sphere** (golf ball to beach ball size) that floats or moves for several seconds, sometimes near a strike. Its cause is still debated. |
| **Sheet lightning** | Lightning **within or behind a cloud** that lights up the cloud as a broad, diffuse flash with no visible channel. |
| **Heat lightning** *(related term)* | Lightning from a storm **too far away for its thunder to be heard**, often seen on summer nights. It is ordinary lightning, not caused by heat. |
| **St. Elmo's Fire** | A steady, bluish-purple **glow (corona discharge)** on pointed objects, such as ship masts, airplane wings, towers, and mountaintops, when the electric field near a storm is strong. It is **not actually lightning**, but its presence means a strike could be nearby. |

#### Upper-Atmospheric Lightning: Blue Jets, Sprites, Elves
These brief flashes occur **above** thunderstorms and are known as **transient luminous events (TLEs)**. Only blue jets are mostly in the stratosphere; sprites and elves occur even higher, in the **mesosphere** and lower **ionosphere**.
- **Blue jets:** Narrow **blue cones** shooting upward from the **top of a thunderstorm** into the **stratosphere** (up to ~40–50 km).
- **Sprites:** Large, faint, **reddish** flashes that look like jellyfish or carrots, in the **mesosphere** (~50–90 km), triggered by strong **positive** cloud-to-ground strikes below. They last only milliseconds.
- **Elves:** Expanding, **doughnut-shaped rings** of faint red light at about **90 km** in the lower ionosphere, lasting less than a millisecond. (ELVES stands for *Emissions of Light and Very low frequency perturbations due to Electromagnetic pulse Sources*.)
- These are hard to see with the naked eye and were confirmed mostly in the 1990s using sensitive cameras on aircraft, the ground, and the Space Shuttle.

#### Lightning Rods
- **Benjamin Franklin** invented the lightning rod in the **1750s**.
- A lightning protection system has **air terminals** (pointed metal rods) on the highest points, **down conductors** (heavy wires), and **grounding rods** buried in the earth, all **bonded** together.
- **How they work:** A lightning rod **does not attract** lightning from miles away. It **intercepts** a strike that would have hit the building anyway and provides a **safe, low-resistance path** to carry the current **into the ground**, so it doesn't pass through the structure (where it could cause fires or damage). This is still how modern, standards-approved systems work today.
- **Charge dissipation:** Franklin originally hoped sharp points would quietly "bleed off" a cloud's charge and **prevent** strikes. Some modern products, sometimes called **charge dissipation arrays** or "lightning prevention" systems, are marketed on this idea. However, scientific studies and national lightning-protection standards **do not support** the claim that any device can prevent lightning, and such systems have been struck. Know both ideas for the exam, but understand that **intercepting and grounding** the current is the accepted method.
- Modern systems also use **surge protectors** to protect electronics from current coming in through power and phone lines.

#### Lightning Safety
- **"When thunder roars, go indoors!"** There is **no safe place outdoors** during a thunderstorm.
- **Safe shelter:** A **substantial building** with plumbing and wiring, or a **hard-topped metal vehicle** with windows closed. (The vehicle protects because the **metal shell** carries current around you, **not** because of the rubber tires.)
- **Unsafe places:** open fields, hilltops, beaches, open water, isolated tall trees, picnic shelters, dugouts, tents, convertibles, golf carts.
- **Wait 30 minutes** after the **last** thunder before going back outside. Lightning often strikes **before** rain arrives and **after** it ends.
- **Indoors:** Stay away from **corded phones**, **plumbing** (showers, sinks), **electrical equipment**, windows, and doors; don't lie on concrete floors.
- **If caught outside with no shelter:** get off elevated areas, stay away from water and metal objects (fences, power lines), and never shelter under a lone tree. Spread out from others in a group. (These steps lower the risk, but nothing outdoors is truly safe.)
- **Ways people are struck:** **direct strike**, **side flash** (jumping from a tree or object to a nearby person), **ground current** (spreading through the ground; causes the most casualties), **conduction** (through wires or metal), and **streamers**.
- **First aid:** Lightning victims **do not carry an electrical charge** and are safe to touch. Call 911 and begin **CPR** if needed; cardiac arrest is the main cause of lightning deaths.

## Chapter 15 – Tornadoes

A **tornado** is a **violently rotating column of air** in contact with the ground and extending from (or beneath) a **cumuliform cloud**, usually a cumulonimbus. A rotating funnel that does **not** reach the ground is a **funnel cloud**. The visible funnel is condensed water droplets plus dust and debris; the circulation on the ground can be present even when the funnel doesn't appear to touch down.

**Basic facts:**
- The U.S. has **more tornadoes than any other country**, on the order of 1,200 per year.
- Peak season is **spring (April–June)** in the southern and central Plains, shifting north in summer; the Southeast (sometimes called "**Dixie Alley**") also has many tornadoes, including in fall, winter, and at night.
- "**Tornado Alley**" is the traditional name for the central Plains (Texas to South Dakota) where cT/cP/mT air masses frequently clash.
- Most tornadoes **rotate counterclockwise** in the NH (cyclonically), are **weak** (EF0–EF1), last **a few minutes**, and are less than 100 m wide. Violent ones can exceed **200 mph**, be over a **mile wide**, and stay on the ground for **an hour or more**.
- Most tornadoes move from **southwest to northeast**, steered by the parent storm, typically at 20–40 mph (sometimes faster).
- Some tornadoes are **multiple-vortex**, with smaller **suction vortices** rotating around the main circulation, which explains narrow paths of extreme damage.

### 15.1 Tornadoes Form During Thunderstorms

#### Largest Tornadoes in Supercell Thunderstorms
- **Supercell tornadoes** form from the storm's **mesocyclone** (Ch. 14.3). Nearly all **strong and violent** tornadoes (EF2–EF5) come from supercells.
- **Simplified formation sequence:**
  1. **Wind shear** creates horizontal rolling (spin) near the ground.
  2. The storm's **updraft tilts** the spin into the vertical → **mesocyclone** (several km wide), often several km above the ground.
  3. The **rear-flank downdraft (RFD)** wraps around the mesocyclone and helps concentrate rotation near the ground.
  4. The rotation is **stretched** vertically and **narrows**, so it **spins faster** (like an ice skater pulling in their arms; conservation of angular momentum), forming a tornado.
- Scientists still cannot fully predict **which** supercells will produce tornadoes; only a fraction of mesocyclones do.

#### Wind Shear and Instability
- **Wind shear:** Especially strong **low-level shear** (in the lowest 1 km) with winds **veering** (turning clockwise) with height, such as southeasterly at the surface and southwesterly/westerly aloft, is strongly linked to tornadoes. Forecasters measure this with **storm-relative helicity**.
- **Instability:** High **CAPE** produces strong updrafts that can stretch the rotation. **Low cloud bases** (humid low levels, small dew point depression) also favor tornadoes.
- **Lift:** fronts, dry lines, and outflow boundaries trigger the storms.
- A common setup: **warm, humid mT air** at the surface from the Gulf, **dry cT air** above it from the Southwest (creating a cap), **cold, dry air** aloft, a strong **jet stream** overhead, and a **low-level jet** feeding moisture.

### 15.2 Dust Devils, Waterspouts, and Landspouts

| Feature | Description | Is it a tornado? |
|---|---|---|
| **Dust devil** | A small, rotating column of air made visible by dust, forming on **clear, sunny, hot days** from intense **surface heating**, often over deserts and plowed fields. Usually short-lived (seconds to minutes) and weak, though some can cause minor damage. | **No.** It forms from the ground up in a superheated layer and is **not connected to a cloud**. |
| **Fair-weather waterspout** | A weak vortex over water that forms from the **water surface upward** beneath a developing cumulus or cumulus congestus, often along a boundary, with **little wind shear**. Common in places like the Florida Keys. Usually brief and weaker than supercell tornadoes. | It is a **non-supercell** tornado-like vortex over water. |
| **Tornadic waterspout** | A true **supercell tornado** over water, or one that moves from land onto water. | **Yes**, and can be dangerous. |
| **Landspout** | A **non-supercell tornado** over land, forming beneath a growing cumulus or thunderstorm where pre-existing **spin along a boundary** (like a front or outflow) is **stretched** by the updraft. Often has a narrow, rope-like, dusty appearance; usually weak and short-lived. Not associated with a mesocyclone, so radar often doesn't detect it. | **Yes**, it is classified as a tornado. |

### 15.3 The Right Side of a Tornado Is Most Damaging

- A tornado's winds combine **two motions**: the **rotational wind** spinning around the center and the **forward (translational) speed** of the whole tornado.
- In the NH, most tornadoes spin **counterclockwise**. On the **right side** relative to the direction of motion, the rotating winds blow in the **same direction** the tornado is moving, so the speeds **add**. On the **left side**, they blow **against** the motion, so the speeds **subtract**.
- *Example:* A tornado with **150 mph** rotational winds moving forward at **30 mph**:
  - Right side: 150 + 30 = **180 mph**
  - Left side: 150 − 30 = **120 mph**
- The same principle applies to **hurricanes** (Ch. 16), whose **right-front quadrant** has the strongest winds and storm surge.

### 15.4 Enhanced Fujita (EF) Scale

- The **EF Scale** rates tornado **intensity** based on the **damage** it causes, since wind speeds inside tornadoes are rarely measured directly.
- Developed from the original **Fujita (F) Scale** (Dr. T. Theodore Fujita, 1971), and adopted by the NWS on **February 1, 2007**, because the F Scale overestimated wind speeds for violent tornadoes and lacked clear damage guidelines.
- NWS meteorologists conduct **damage surveys** after a tornado. They use **28 damage indicators (DIs)** (such as one- and two-family homes, mobile homes, schools, towers, and hardwood and softwood trees), each with several **degrees of damage (DODs)**, to estimate the wind speed. Construction quality matters: a poorly built house destroyed by lower winds doesn't earn the same rating as a well-built one.
- A tornado is rated by the **most intense damage** along its path.

| EF Rating | Estimated 3-second gust | Typical damage |
|---|---|---|
| **EF0** | 65–85 mph | Light: shingles and gutters damaged, branches broken, shallow-rooted trees pushed over |
| **EF1** | 86–110 mph | Moderate: roofs badly stripped, mobile homes overturned, windows broken |
| **EF2** | 111–135 mph | Considerable: roofs torn off frame houses, mobile homes destroyed, large trees snapped or uprooted |
| **EF3** | 136–165 mph | Severe: entire stories of well-built houses destroyed, heavy cars lifted, trees debarked |
| **EF4** | 166–200 mph | Devastating: well-built houses leveled, cars thrown |
| **EF5** | Over 200 mph | Incredible: strong frame houses swept off foundations, steel-reinforced concrete structures badly damaged |

- **Weak:** EF0–EF1; **Strong:** EF2–EF3; **Violent:** EF4–EF5 (only about 1–2% of tornadoes, but responsible for a large share of deaths).
- If a tornado hits nothing that can be rated (an open field), it may be rated **EF-Unknown (EF-U)**.
- Because the scale is based on damage, it rates what the tornado **did**, not necessarily the strongest winds it had. Researchers are working on an updated standard that adds other methods (like radar measurements and tree-fall patterns) for estimating tornado winds.

### 15.5 Hook Echo (Radar Signatures)

- On **reflectivity** radar images, a **hook echo** is a **hook- or comma-shaped appendage** curling from the **rear (usually southwest) flank** of a supercell.
- It forms as **precipitation is wrapped around the mesocyclone** by the rear-flank downdraft. The tornado, if present, is usually near the **tip/inside curl** of the hook.
- A hook echo indicates a rotating storm that **may** produce a tornado; not every hook echo has one, and tornadoes in heavy rain (HP supercells) or landspouts may not show a clear hook.
- **Other radar clues:**
  - **Velocity couplet (rotation):** On **Doppler velocity** images, strong **inbound** winds (usually green) right next to strong **outbound** winds (usually red) indicate rotation. A tight, intense couplet is called a **tornado vortex signature (TVS)**.
  - **Debris ball / tornado debris signature (TDS):** A small area of high reflectivity at the hook's tip, and on **dual-polarization** radar, **low correlation coefficient**, means the radar is detecting **lofted debris**, confirming a damaging tornado on the ground.
  - **Bounded weak echo region (BWER):** marks a very strong updraft.

#### Tornado Safety (Bonus)
- During a **Tornado Warning**, go to the **lowest floor**, in an **interior room** without windows (closet, bathroom, hallway), and **cover your head**; a basement or storm shelter/safe room is best.
- **Mobile homes and vehicles are not safe**; go to a sturdy building or shelter in advance.
- **Highway overpasses are dangerous** shelters (winds are funneled and debris flies through).
- Don't waste time opening windows; it doesn't help.

## Chapter 16 – Tropical Cyclones (Hurricanes, Typhoons, Cyclones)

A **tropical cyclone** is a rotating, organized system of thunderstorms that forms over **warm tropical oceans**, with a **closed low-level circulation**. Unlike mid-latitude cyclones (Ch. 12), tropical cyclones have **no fronts**, are **warm-core** (warmer in the center than the surroundings), and are powered by the **latent heat** released when water vapor evaporated from the warm ocean condenses in their thunderstorms. They are essentially giant **heat engines**.

**Same storm, different names by location:**
- **Hurricane:** North Atlantic, Caribbean, Gulf of Mexico, and the eastern and central North Pacific
- **Typhoon:** Northwest Pacific (east Asia)
- **Cyclone:** Indian Ocean and South Pacific (e.g., "severe tropical cyclone" near Australia)

Tropical cyclones rotate **counterclockwise in the NH** and **clockwise in the SH**.

### 16.1 Structure: Eye, Eyewall, Rainbands, Outflow Divergence

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Eye** | The **calm center**, typically **20–50 km (≈10–30 mi)** across. Air **sinks** and warms by compression, so skies are often **clear or partly cloudy** and winds are **light**. Has the **lowest surface pressure** in the storm. Danger: people sometimes go outside during the eye's calm, then get caught when the other side of the eyewall arrives with winds from the **opposite direction**. |
| **Eyewall** | A ring of towering **cumulonimbus** surrounding the eye with the **strongest winds**, **heaviest rain**, and most intense upward motion. Strong hurricanes may go through an **eyewall replacement cycle**, in which a new outer eyewall forms and replaces the inner one (the storm often weakens temporarily, then grows larger). |
| **Spiral rainbands** | Curved bands of showers and thunderstorms spiraling **inward** toward the eyewall, sometimes hundreds of km from the center. They bring bursts of heavy rain and gusty winds, with calmer gaps between them, and can spawn **tornadoes** (especially in the **right-front quadrant**). |
| **Outflow (divergence aloft)** | At the top of the storm (upper troposphere), air **spreads outward**, turning **clockwise** (anticyclonically, in the NH), forming a broad **cirrus shield**. This **upper-level divergence** removes the air that converges and rises at low levels, allowing the **surface pressure to keep falling** and the storm to strengthen (same principle as Ch. 12.4). |

**Overall circulation:** Air **converges** and spirals **inward** at the surface (counterclockwise in the NH), **rises** in the eyewall and rainbands releasing latent heat, and **diverges outward** at the top. A small amount sinks in the eye.

### 16.2 Conditions for Formation

#### Warm Water
- Ocean surface temperatures of at least about **26.5 °C (80 °F)**, extending to a depth of about **50 m (150 ft)**, so the storm doesn't churn up cold water from below.
- Warm water supplies **heat and moisture** (the storm's fuel). This is why tropical cyclones **weaken rapidly over land or cold water**: they lose their energy source (and over land, friction also slows the winds).
- The atmosphere must also be **unstable** and have **moist mid-levels**; dry air (such as dust-laden air from the Sahara) weakens developing storms.

#### Low Pressure (Trigger)
- A **pre-existing disturbance** is needed to start the process: an area of low pressure with converging surface winds and thunderstorms.
- In the Atlantic, the most common trigger is a **tropical wave (easterly wave)**, a trough of low pressure moving **westward off the coast of Africa** in the trade winds. Others include the **ITCZ**, old frontal boundaries, and upper-level lows.

#### Light Winds (Weak Vertical Wind Shear)
- Winds should change **little in speed or direction with height**.
- **Strong vertical wind shear** tilts the storm and blows the thunderstorm tops (and released heat) **away from the low-level center**, tearing the storm apart. Low shear lets the storm stack vertically and concentrate its heat.
- This is the **opposite** of supercells and tornadoes (Ch. 14–15), which **need** strong shear.
- **El Niño** increases wind shear over the Atlantic (fewer Atlantic hurricanes); **La Niña** decreases it (more Atlantic hurricanes) (connection to Ch. 10).

#### Coriolis Effect
- Needed to start the **rotation**. Because the Coriolis force is **zero at the equator**, tropical cyclones almost never form **within about 5° of the equator**, and they never cross it.
- Most form between about **5° and 20°** latitude.

#### Hurricane Season
- **Atlantic** (including the Caribbean and Gulf of Mexico): **June 1 – November 30**, with a **peak around September 10** (most activity August–October, when ocean water is warmest).
- **Eastern North Pacific:** May 15 – November 30. **Central North Pacific:** June 1 – November 30.
- Storms can occasionally form outside the official season.

### 16.3 Classification and the Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale

Tropical cyclones are classified by their **maximum sustained winds** (1-minute average, about 10 m above the surface).

#### Stages of Development

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| **Tropical disturbance** | An organized cluster of thunderstorms persisting at least **24 hours**, with **no closed circulation** (often a tropical wave). |
| **Tropical depression** | A **closed circulation** forms; maximum sustained winds **38 mph (33 kt) or less**. Gets a **number** (e.g., Tropical Depression Five). |
| **Tropical storm** | Sustained winds **39–73 mph (34–63 kt)**. Gets a **name**. Storm becomes more organized and circular. |
| **Hurricane** | Sustained winds **74 mph (64 kt) or greater**. An eye usually forms. Rated on the Saffir-Simpson Scale. |

#### Saffir-Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale

*Note: Strictly speaking, the Saffir-Simpson Scale covers only **Categories 1–5** (hurricanes). The disturbance, depression, and storm stages above are classifications used by the National Hurricane Center before a storm reaches hurricane strength.*

| Category | Sustained winds | Expected damage |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | 74–95 mph (64–82 kt) | Very dangerous winds will produce **some damage**: roofs, shingles, gutters, large tree branches; power outages for several days |
| **2** | 96–110 mph (83–95 kt) | Extremely dangerous winds will cause **extensive damage**: major roof and siding damage, many trees uprooted, near-total power loss for days to weeks |
| **3 (major)** | 111–129 mph (96–112 kt) | **Devastating damage**: well-built homes may lose roof decking and gable ends; electricity and water unavailable for days to weeks |
| **4 (major)** | 130–156 mph (113–136 kt) | **Catastrophic damage**: severe damage to well-built homes, most trees snapped or uprooted, area uninhabitable for weeks or months |
| **5 (major)** | 157 mph or higher (137+ kt) | **Catastrophic damage**: a high percentage of framed homes destroyed; area uninhabitable for weeks or months |

- **Major hurricane** = **Category 3 or higher**.
- The scale is based on **wind only**. It does **not** account for **storm surge, rainfall flooding, or tornadoes**, which cause most hurricane deaths. A lower-category storm can still be extremely deadly (for example, a slow-moving storm with extreme rain).
- **Rapid intensification:** an increase of at least **35 mph (30 kt) in 24 hours**, which is difficult to forecast and dangerous near the coast.

### 16.4 Naming Hurricanes

- Names make it easier to **communicate** about storms, especially when more than one is active at the same time, and reduce confusion in warnings.
- The **World Meteorological Organization (WMO)** maintains the name lists for each ocean basin.
- **Atlantic basin rules:**
  - **Six lists** of names that **rotate**, so each list is reused every **six years**.
  - Names are in **alphabetical order** and **alternate between male and female** names.
  - Each list has **21 names**; the letters **Q, U, X, Y, and Z** are **not used** because there aren't enough common names starting with them.
  - A storm gets a name when it becomes a **tropical storm** (39 mph).
  - Names of especially **deadly or costly** storms are **retired** and replaced (examples: Katrina, Sandy, Maria, Ian).
  - If all 21 names are used in one season, a **supplemental list** of names is used. (The Greek alphabet was used for this in 2005 and 2020 but was discontinued in 2021 because it caused confusion.)
- **History:** U.S. forecasters began using **female** names in **1953**; **male** names were added in **1979**.
- Other basins use their own lists; in the Northwest Pacific, names are contributed by countries in the region and include words for animals, plants, and other things, not only personal names.

### 16.5 Storm Surge

- **Storm surge** is an **abnormal rise of sea water** above the normally predicted astronomical tide, generated by a storm.
- **Causes:**
  - Mainly, the storm's **strong winds pushing water toward the shore** and piling it up.
  - A smaller contribution from the **low pressure** in the storm's center, which lets the ocean surface bulge upward slightly.
- **Storm tide** = storm surge **+** the normal astronomical tide. A surge arriving at **high tide** is worse.
- **Factors that make surge higher:** stronger winds, **larger storm size**, **storm forward speed and angle** of approach to the coast, a **shallow, gently sloping continental shelf** (like the Gulf Coast), and the shape of the coastline (water funnels into **bays and inlets**).
- Storm surge has historically been the **deadliest** hurricane hazard (e.g., the 1900 Galveston hurricane, Katrina in 2005). In recent decades, **inland freshwater flooding from heavy rain** has also caused many deaths.
- NHC issues **Storm Surge Watches and Warnings** for life-threatening surge. Evacuation advice: "**Run from the water, hide from the wind.**"

#### Front Right Side
- In the NH, the **right-front quadrant** (relative to the storm's direction of motion) typically has the **highest storm surge** and **strongest winds**.
- **Why:** On the right side, the storm's **counterclockwise winds blow in the same direction as the storm is moving**, so the wind speed and forward speed **add** (same principle as tornadoes, Ch. 15.3). In front of the storm on the right side, those strong winds blow **onshore**, pushing water toward the coast.
  - *Example:* A hurricane with 100 mph winds moving forward at 15 mph → about **115 mph** on the right side and about **85 mph** on the left side.
- On the **left side**, winds may blow **offshore**, which can actually **lower** the water level temporarily.
- The **right-front quadrant** is also where **tornadoes** in rainbands are most common.

### 16.6 The Forecast Cone (Cone of Uncertainty)

- The National Hurricane Center's **track forecast cone** shows the **probable track of the center** of the storm, not its size.
- **How it's made:** A circle is placed at each forecast point (12, 24, 36, 48, 72, 96, and 120 hours), sized so that about **two-thirds (≈67%)** of historical official forecast errors from the previous **five years** fall inside it. Connecting the circles forms the cone. The cone **widens** with time because forecasts become less certain further out.
- **What it means:** The storm's center is expected to stay inside the cone about **two-thirds of the time**, so about **one-third of the time** the center moves **outside** the cone.
- **Common misunderstandings:**
  - The cone does **not** show the storm's **size**. Hurricane- and tropical-storm-force winds, storm surge, heavy rain, and tornadoes often extend **far outside** the cone.
  - Being **outside** the cone does not mean you are safe. Being near the **centerline** isn't the only place at risk.
  - The cone is **not** a zone of impacts; check watches, warnings, and local NWS forecasts for impacts.
- Track forecasts have improved greatly over recent decades, so today's cones are much **narrower** than they used to be. **Intensity** forecasts are still harder than track forecasts.
- **Hurricane Watch:** hurricane conditions **possible** within the area, usually issued **48 hours** before tropical-storm-force winds are expected. **Hurricane Warning:** hurricane conditions **expected**, usually issued **36 hours** in advance.

### Mid-Latitude Cyclone vs. Tropical Cyclone

| | Mid-latitude cyclone | Tropical cyclone |
|---|---|---|
| Where it forms | Along the **polar front**, middle latitudes | Over **warm tropical oceans** |
| Fronts? | **Yes** (warm, cold, occluded) | **No** |
| Energy source | **Horizontal temperature contrast** between air masses | **Latent heat** from warm ocean water |
| Core | **Cold-core** (colder aloft at center) | **Warm-core** |
| Wind shear | Needs a **strong jet stream** / shear | Needs **weak** shear |
| Strongest winds | Spread over a large area, often aloft (jet stream) | Near the **surface**, in the **eyewall** |
| Most common season (U.S.) | **Winter and spring** | **Late summer and fall** |

### Thunderstorm Types and Wind Shear

| Storm type | Wind shear | Lifespan | Severe weather? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary (single) cell | Weak | 30–60 min | Rarely; brief heavy rain, small hail, microbursts |
| Multicell (clusters, squall lines, MCCs, derechos) | Moderate | Hours | Sometimes; damaging winds, hail, flash floods, brief tornadoes |
| Supercell | Strong | Several hours | Often; large hail, damaging winds, strong/violent tornadoes |
