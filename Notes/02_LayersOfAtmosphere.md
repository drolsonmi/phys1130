In order to really understand what it looks like, we need to understand two physics principles: Density and Pressure

## __Physics__: Density
__Density__ is a measure of how much mass is crammed into a space. For example, the Density of water is $1000 kg/m^3$, or $1 g/cm^3$. That means $1 cm^3$ of water has $1g$ of mass crammed into it.
- What would happen if more mass is crammed into the same volume?
    - If any mass has more mass than 1g in a $cm^3$, then it will sink
- What would happen if there is less mass in that same volume?
    - If any mass has more less than 1g in a $cm^3$, then it will float

> __Demo__: Bottle with density beads

To calculate density,

$$density = \frac{mass}{volume}$$

>     - Find the weight and calculate density
>     - Which will sink? Which will float?

Our atmosphere is a fluid as well and behaves like water in this way. As a result, 
- Any individual molecules that have less density than the air will float to the top of the atmosphere
    - Hydrogen and Helium
- Any individual molecules that have more density than the air will sink to the surface
    - Water, Oxygen, Carbon Dioxide, etc.
    - Heaviest molecules (like lead) will fall *all the way* to the surface and be removed from the atmosphere

Where would the density of the atmosphere be greatest? Where the heaviest molecules gather or where the lightest molecules gather?
- Heaviest molecules = greatest density
- Density of the air is greatest at the surface and decreases as we increase in elevation

### Density Profile of the Atmosphere
* Most dense gases will sink and collect near the surface. Least dense gases will float and remain higher in the atmosphere
* Density is thus highest near the surface and drops off exponentially


## __Physics__: Pressure
__Pressure__ is a measure of the distribution of force over an area.

$$pressure = \frac{force}{area}$$

How does the pressure change in each of these cases?
* If the force increases?
* If the force is applied over a smaller area? over a larger area?
* If the force is distributed over two scales instead of one?

> __Demo__: Object distributed over many scales vs. all on one scale

The atmosphere has weight. In fact, every square inch of the surface of the Earth (and on us!) is feeling 14.7 pounds of force from the weight of the atmosphere. Atmosphere exerts a pressure of:
* 14.7 psi
* 29.92 inHg
* 101,325 Pa
* 1013.25 hPa = 1013.25 mb

> __Demo__: JetStream: A Pressing Engagement (demonstration of air pressure)
> * Why does the printer paper pop up easily but the newspaper does not?
>     * Air fills in under the printer paper, balancing the pressure on each side
>     * Air does not fill uner the newspaper as quickly, so there's an imbalance of pressure with the atmosphere pushing down a little harder, making it more difficult to lift

### Pressure Profile of the Atmosphere
Pressure is based on force. Air pressure is the result of the weight of the atmosphere pushing down on the surface.

Consider a swimming pool
* If you put your head underwater, you feel a little pressure
* If you dive a little, the pressure increases slightly (weight of water on top of you)
* As you dive deeping, the pressure increases dramatically (collective weight of water on top of you)

The pressure would thus increase linearly, or maybe slightly exponentially, as you dive deeper. The same thing happens with the atmosphere.
* Near the top, you have very little weight on top of you, so the pressure is small
* As you approach the surface, the pressure increases because of the collective weight of the atmosphere
    * However, since the density is higher nearer the surface, the cumulative weight increases MUCH more as you approach the surface

The result is another exponential pressure profile that mirrors the density profile

#### Pressure and Height
What would be the pressure if we could ascend into the atmosphere?
* Surface, roughly 1000 mb (actual number is 1013 mb at sea level)
* Ephraim is at an altitude of 5,542 feet (1,689 m). This is a pressure of 830 mb.
* 700 mb is around 3,000 m (10,000 ft or 1.9 miles)
    * 70% of a standard atmosphere above you
* 500 mb is around 5,500 m (18,000 ft or 3.4 miles)
    * 50% of a standard atmosphere above you
    * The midpoint of the atmosphere
* 300 mb is around 9,000 m (30,000 ft or 5.7 miles) - commercial jet cruising altitude
    * 30% of a standard atmosphere above you (70% below you)
    * Peak of Mt. Everest
    * Top of the troposphere (lowest layer of the atmosphere)

I often show 700-mb and 500-mb charts when doing forecasts at the beginning of class.

## Temperature Profile of the Atmosphere
Air does not absorb sunlight very well at all. As a result, it heats up more on the ground than in the air itself. The lowest layer of the atmosphere following this pattern is known as the __troposphere__.
* If you went up in altitude, the temperature would drop
* Temperature drops at an average rate of 3.5$^\circ F$ every 1,000ft or 6.5$^\circ C/km$

As we continue up into the atmosphere, we are going to encounter two other types of layers:
* An __isothermal__ layer is a layer of the atmosphere where temperature doesn't change (iso = same, thermal = temperature)
* An __inversion__ layer is a layer of the atmosphere that does the opposite of what we expect - the temperature *increases* with height

At the top of the troposphere, temperature actually stops changes and, for a little while, temperature is relatively uniform. This layer is called the __tropopause__. (about 11 km, 36,000 ft, or 230 mb)

Above the tropopause, we have a layer filled with gases that readily absorb high-energy ultraviolet light. This layer is the __ozone layer__, and it protects us at the surface from this potentially dangerous light. As a result, this layer of the atmosphere heats up because of the added energy. This *inversion* layer is known as the __stratosphere__.

Above the stratosphere, the gases stop effectively absorbing high-energy radiation. The density is so low that there is little air to actually heat up. As a result, the temperature stops dropping and we have another *isothermal* layer: the __stratopause__. (about 50 km, 160,000 ft, or 1.0 mb)

Above the stratopause we have the __mesosphere__, a region of the atmosphere whose temperature dramatically decreases with altitude.

Above the mesosphere is another *isothermal* layer: the __mesopause__. (85 km, 279,000 ft, or 0.01 mb)

Above the mesopause, any molecules that are left are exposed to direct, unfiltered sunlight. As a result, those molecules absorb all the energy from sunlight that they can. The result is that the temperature of those individual molecules increases dramatically. The result is another *inversion* layer: the __thermosphere__.

* Altitude of the International Space Station: 400 km (250 miles) - technically still in the thermosphere

### The Ionosphere
* Thermosphere, Mesosphere, and part of the Stratosphere get bombarded with so much radiation that the molecules there are *ionized* (electrons are stripped from the molecules)
* This region of ionized gases is known at the __ionosphere__
* Can affect communication - AM radio waves travel further at night
* Most intense during the day, weaker at night
* Study of the ionosphere is in the field of [*Space Weather*](./05_SpaceWx_Seasons.md)
