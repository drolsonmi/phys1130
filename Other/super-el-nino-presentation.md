# Abstract

The news is abuzz with the **Super El Niño** that is hitting us! But what is a Super El Niño? Will it cause snow for us this winter? How will the El Niño season affect us?

We will discuss what El Niño is and how it will affect our weather here in Utah.

---

# The Headlines

- "Super El Niño is coming"
- "Potentially historic"
- "Strongest on record?"
- Ski resorts announcing early opening dates
- Hopes for a rebound after last winter's **record-low Utah snowpack**

**Today's questions:**
1. What is actually being forecast?
2. Does El Niño *cause* our weather, or just *correlate* with it?
3. How does El Niño work?
4. What does it mean for the West — and for Utah?

# Part 1
## The Forecast

---

# Where We Stand (CPC, Oct 8, 2026)

**ENSO Alert Status: El Niño Advisory**

- El Niño **strengthened again** last month
- Sea surface temperature anomalies **> +4.0 °C** in parts of the eastern equatorial Pacific
- Monthly index values:

| Region | Anomaly |
|---|---|
| Niño 3.4 (the "official" region) | **+2.1 °C** |
| Niño 3 | +3.0 °C |
| Niño 1+2 (off South America) | +3.9 °C |

- Latest **weekly** Niño 3.4 value (Oct 15): **+2.4 °C**

<!--
Source: CPC ENSO Diagnostic Discussion, Oct 8, 2026; weekly value from the CPC seasonal outlook discussion issued Oct 15, 2026.
Point out: Niño 1+2 is right off the coast of Peru/Ecuador — that's where the original "El Niño" warm current was noticed by fishermen.
-->

---

# Below the Surface

- Subsurface temperatures **more than +10 °C above normal** at depth in the eastern Pacific
- Thermocline is **deeper than average**
- A large reservoir of warm water extends from ~170°W to South America, **deeper than 200 m**

➡️ CPC: there is "considerable remaining fuel" for further strengthening

![bg right:45% fit](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/analysis_monitoring/enso_advisory/figure04.gif)

<!--
Analogy: the surface temperature is the flame you can see; the subsurface warm pool is the fuel tank. Right now the tank is still full.
Figure: CPC ENSO Discussion Fig. 4 (subsurface temperature anomalies).
-->

---

# The Atmosphere Is Responding Too

El Niño is a **coupled** ocean–atmosphere event. Signs the atmosphere has "locked in":

- Low-level **westerly** wind anomalies → trade winds substantially weakened
- **Enhanced** rainfall/convection in the central & eastern Pacific
- **Suppressed** rainfall over Indonesia
- Strongly **negative** Southern Oscillation Index
- Very active central/eastern Pacific hurricane season; suppressed Atlantic season

<!--
The hurricane signal is a nice "you've seen this" moment: the remnants of former Hurricane/Tropical Storm Rachel brought heavy rain to Utah earlier this month. An active East Pacific hurricane season is a classic El Niño fingerprint.
-->

---

# How Strong Is "Strong"?

NOAA classifies El Niño by the 3-month Niño 3.4 index (now the **Relative** Oceanic Niño Index, RONI):

| Category | Index |
|---|---|
| Weak | +0.5 to +1.0 °C |
| Moderate | +1.0 to +1.5 °C |
| Strong | +1.5 to +2.0 °C |
| **Very Strong** | **≥ +2.0 °C** |

⚠️ **"Super El Niño" is not an official NOAA category.** It's media/forecaster shorthand for "very strong."

<!--
RONI: CPC now measures Niño 3.4 relative to the average of the whole tropics. Why? The entire ocean is warming, so a "raw" anomaly would make every El Niño look bigger over time. RONI isolates the part of the warming that is actually ENSO. This is a great teachable moment about measurement choices in science.
-->

---

# The Forecast: How Big Will It Get?

- Forecast models peak Niño 3.4 near **+3.0 to +3.5 °C** in Nov–Jan
- **>80% chance** of a strong-to-very-strong El Niño through **Jan–Mar 2027**
- Chance of a **historic** event (RONI ≥ +2.5 °C — stronger than any El Niño since 1950):

| Season | Chance |
|---|---|
| Sep–Nov | 54% |
| **Oct–Dec** | **83%** |
| Nov–Jan | 70% |

- Then: ENSO-neutral by **summer 2027**, possible **La Niña in autumn 2027**

<!--
Sources: CPC ENSO Discussion (Oct 8) and CPC long-lead seasonal discussion (Oct 15).
Emphasize: forecasters are very confident about the OCEAN. The uncertainty is in what the ocean does to OUR weather — that's the next section.
-->

---

# Putting It in Historical Context

The strongest El Niños of the modern record:

| Event | Nickname / Notes |
|---|---|
| 1982–83 | Caught forecasters by surprise; massive global impacts |
| 1997–98 | "El Niño of the century" (at the time) |
| 2015–16 | "Godzilla El Niño" |
| **2026–27** | **Forecast to rival or exceed all of them** |

> One local forecaster suggested it could be the strongest El Niño in centuries.

<!--
Note that 1997–98 and 2015–16 both happened during a POSITIVE Pacific Decadal Oscillation; this year the PDO is NEGATIVE. CPC says that makes the PDO's influence hard to judge, though it is likely minimal given El Niño's strength. Key message: we don't have a perfect historical analog for this one — and it's happening on top of a warmer climate.
-->

# Part 2
## Causation vs. Correlation

---

# El Niño Doesn't *Make* It Snow

**Causation (physical chain):**
Warm tropical Pacific → shifted thunderstorms → altered jet stream → shifted storm track

**Correlation (statistical result):**
"In El Niño winters, Region X has *tended* to be wetter."

El Niño **loads the dice**. It doesn't decide the roll.

- Any individual storm is still weather — driven by day-to-day patterns
- El Niño shifts the **odds** of seasonal outcomes, region by region

<!--
Analogy: El Niño is like a weighted die. If a die is weighted toward 6, you'll see more 6s over many rolls — but any single roll can still be a 1. And we only get ONE roll per winter.
-->

---

# The Small-Sample Problem

- Since 1950, there are only **a handful** of very strong El Niños
- Averages can be skewed by **one or two** extreme winters

**Example — Central Sierra Snow Lab (California):**
- Above-average snow in **about half** of El Niño winters since 1950, below average in the other half
- Of the four strongest El Niños: **2 above normal, 2 below**

**Example — "Godzilla" 2015–16:**
- Expected: wet Southern California
- Actual: **drier than normal** in SoCal & Arizona; wet across Nevada

<!--
Source: USGS Southwest Climate Adaptation Science Center, "Regional Super El Niño Impacts: Southwest U.S." (2026).
Ask the audience: "If I flip a coin 4 times and get 3 heads, have I proven the coin is biased?" That's roughly the statistical situation for very strong El Niños.
-->

---

# Utah's Coin Flip

Utah's Assistant State Climatologist (KUER, Sept 2026):

- Urged people to **"pump the brakes"** on the hype
- Historically, ENSO has had **little push** on how Utah winters end up
- Past strong El Niños gave Utah **as many below-average winters as above-average**

**Why?** Utah sits in the **"no man's land"** between El Niño's wet South and dry North.

<!--
This is the central slide for the "correlation" argument. The signal is strong in Southern California, Arizona, the Gulf Coast, and Florida — and weak in Utah because we straddle the dividing line.
-->

---

# Other Things Muddy the Signal

- **Atmospheric rivers** — big precipitation producers in the West, but El Niño is a poor predictor of where/when they hit
- **Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO)** — currently negative, a phase that historically hasn't paired well with good Utah winters
- **Stratospheric polar vortex** — westerly QBO + El Niño favor a stable vortex → fewer Arctic cold outbreaks
- **Climate change** — a warmer baseline raises the rain/snow line

**Bottom line:** ENSO is one influence among several — a strong one, but not the only one.

# Part 3
## What Makes an El Niño?

---

# Where the Name Comes From

- *El Niño* = Spanish for "the boy," a reference to the Christ child
- Named by **Peruvian fishermen** for a warm current that appeared after Christmas
- Brought **poor fishing** along the coast and **heavy rain** to normally dry land

Later research revealed it was part of something bigger: **ENSO — El Niño/Southern Oscillation**

<!--
Source: NOAA JetStream, "El Niño/Southern Oscillation (ENSO)."
-->

---

# Normal Conditions

![bg right:50% fit](https://www.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-09/neutral.png)

- **Trade winds** blow east → west along the equator
- Warm surface water piles up in the **western Pacific**
  - Sea level ~**1.5 ft (46 cm) higher** near Indonesia than Peru
  - Western Pacific ~**14 °F (8 °C) warmer** than off South America
- Off South America: cold, nutrient-rich water **upwells** from below
- Rising air & rain over Indonesia; sinking, dry air over the eastern Pacific

This loop is called the **Walker Circulation**.

<!--
Source: NOAA JetStream, "Effects of ENSO in the Pacific."
Draw it on the whiteboard if possible: wind arrows east→west at the surface, rising air over Indonesia, return flow aloft, sinking over the eastern Pacific.
-->

---

# The Thermocline

The **thermocline** is the boundary between warm surface water and cold deep water.

| | Western Pacific | Eastern Pacific |
|---|---|---|
| Normal depth | ~450 ft (150 m) | ~90 ft (30 m) |

- A **shallow** eastern thermocline lets winds pull up cold water easily
- That cold upwelling keeps the eastern Pacific cool and productive for fisheries

<!--
Analogy: imagine a bathtub with a layer of warm water on top of cold water. Blow across the surface from one end, and the warm layer piles up at the far end and thins at your end — the cold water is right there near the surface.
-->

---

# El Niño Conditions

![bg right:50% fit](https://www.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-09/el1.png)

1. Air pressure pattern flips — **higher at Darwin, lower at Tahiti**
2. Trade winds **weaken** (or even reverse)
3. Warm water sloshes back **east**
4. Eastern thermocline **deepens** → upwelling shuts down
5. Eastern Pacific surface **warms**
6. Rainfall shifts from Indonesia toward the **central/eastern Pacific**

---

# The Feedback Loop (Why It Grows)

**Bjerknes feedback:**

Warmer east Pacific → weaker east–west temperature contrast → weaker trade winds → less upwelling → **even warmer** east Pacific → …

- This positive feedback is why El Niño can grow so large
- It's also why this year's deep warm reservoir matters: there's plenty of fuel for the loop

<!--
Ties back to slide 7 ("considerable remaining fuel").
-->

---

# The "Southern Oscillation"

![bg right:40% fit](https://www.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-09/soi_locations.jpg)

- A seesaw in **air pressure** between **Tahiti** and **Darwin, Australia**
- Measured by the **Southern Oscillation Index (SOI)**
- Normal: low pressure at Darwin, high at Tahiti → strong trade winds
- El Niño: the seesaw tips the other way → **negative SOI**

ENSO = the **ocean** part (El Niño/La Niña) + the **atmosphere** part (Southern Oscillation)

---

# How We Measure It

![bg right:45% fit](https://www.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-09/elninozones.jpg)

Sea surface temperatures are tracked in boxes along the equator:

- **Niño 1+2** — off Peru/Ecuador
- **Niño 3** — eastern Pacific
- **Niño 3.4** — east-central Pacific (**the primary index**)
- **Niño 4** — western-central Pacific

Plus buoys, Argo floats, and satellites.

🛰️ Live map: NASA Earth Observatory SST anomalies

<!--
Show the NASA Earth Observatory Sea Surface Temperature Anomaly map live if you have internet: https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/global-maps/sea-surface-temperature-anomaly/
The "tongue" of red along the equator should be unmistakable this year.
Remind the audience: these are changes of only a few degrees, yet they reorganize weather worldwide.
-->

---

# La Niña: The Flip Side

![bg right:50% fit](https://www.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-09/la1.png)

- Trade winds blow **stronger** than normal
- Even more warm water piles up in the west
- **Stronger upwelling** → eastern Pacific **cooler** than normal
- Rainfall shifts **farther west**

El Niño = warm phase · La Niña = cool phase · Neutral = in between

---

# The ENSO Cycle

![bg right:45% fit](https://www.noaa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-11/enso_wheel.jpg)

- ENSO swings irregularly, roughly every **2–7 years**
- Events typically **peak in Northern Hemisphere winter**
- Strong El Niños are often followed by La Niña

**This cycle:**
- El Niño peaks ~Nov–Jan 2026–27
- Neutral favored by summer 2027
- CPC tools hint at **La Niña in autumn 2027**

<!--
La Niña typically favors a wetter northern tier (including, sometimes, northern Utah) and a drier South. So 2027–28 could look very different from 2026–27.
-->

# Part 4
## Weather Impacts

---

# How a Warm Ocean Moves the Jet Stream

1. Warm water fuels **massive thunderstorms** in the central/eastern Pacific
2. That rising air disturbs the upper atmosphere like a rock in a stream
3. The **Pacific jet stream** strengthens and shifts **south and east**
4. Storms follow the jet → the **storm track shifts south**

> The NWS director said a very strong El Niño "tilts the odds toward stormier weather across the South."

---

# Typical Global Impacts

| Region | Typical El Niño signal |
|---|---|
| Coastal Peru & Ecuador | Heavy rain, flooding; poor fishing |
| Indonesia & Australia | Drought, wildfire risk |
| Central & Eastern Pacific | Enhanced hurricanes |
| Atlantic | Suppressed hurricanes |
| Global average | Temperatures **rise** (heat released from ocean) |

<!--
Source: NOAA JetStream ENSO pages and ENSO Weather Impacts page.
Global temperature point: big El Niños release heat into the atmosphere, so 2027 is a strong candidate for a record-warm year globally.
-->

---

# Typical U.S. Winter Pattern

**Wetter** — Southern tier: California, the Southwest, Gulf Coast, **Florida & Southeast** (most reliable signal)

**Drier** — Pacific Northwest, Northern Rockies, Ohio Valley / Great Lakes

**Warmer** — Northern tier (fewer Arctic outbreaks)

**Cooler / near normal** — Southeast (more clouds & rain)

<!--
Consider showing the climate.gov ENSO impacts maps here: https://www.climate.gov/enso
-->

---

# NOAA Winter Outlook (Issued Oct 15, 2026)

**Precipitation**
- **Wetter**: California (40–60% odds statewide), Arizona & New Mexico, Colorado, southern Plains, Southeast
- **Drier**: Northern Rockies (esp. NW Montana), parts of the Great Lakes

**Temperature**
- **Warmer**: Northern tier; strongest in Washington & northern Idaho
- **Equal chances**: interior Southwest — **including Utah**

**Drought**: improvement favored in the Southwest; worsening favored in the northern Rockies

![bg right:38% fit](https://www.cpc.ncep.noaa.gov/products/predictions/long_range/lead02/off02_prcp.gif)

<!--

-->

---

# Western U.S. Risks — Not Just Rewards

A wet El Niño winter can bring:

- 🌊 **Flooding & debris flows** — especially on burn scars in California
- 🏔️ **Rain-on-snow events** → avalanches, rapid runoff, erosion
- 🔥 **Next summer's fire risk** — wet winters grow grass and brush that later dry out
- 🌀 **Autumn tropical moisture** from an active East Pacific hurricane season
- ❄️➡️💧 **Higher snow levels** — warmth means more rain, less snow at low/mid elevations

<!--
Source: USGS Southwest CASC, "Regional Super El Niño Impacts: Southwest U.S."
-->

# Part 5
## What It Means for Us

---

# Utah: Split Down the Middle

- **Southern Utah** — sits closer to El Niño's wet zone
  - Often above-average snow in El Niño winters, sometimes **well** above
  - Best odds in the state this year
- **Northern Utah / Wasatch** — near the dividing line
  - Historically **average to slightly below** in strong El Niños, especially early
- **Official NOAA Dec–Feb outlook:** **Equal chances** for both temperature and precipitation in Utah

<!--
Sources: Ski Utah / OpenSnow (July 2026); KSL & Deseret News (Aug 2026); NOAA CPC outlook (Oct 15, 2026).
Equal chances ≠ "normal winter." It means the tools don't favor any outcome — a wet, dry, or normal winter are about equally likely.
-->

---

# Timing: A Slow Start, Then...?

- Forecasters expect a possible **slow November–December** in northern Utah
- Early-winter precipitation odds stay **south of Utah**
- The **January–March** window looks more favorable
- Spring outlooks keep a wet signal over the Southwest and **central Rockies**

> One forecaster's advice for northern Utah: "trust the process."

<!--
Sources: KUER (Sept 2026), Ski Utah blog, CPC outlook evolution through MAM 2027.
-->

---

# The Temperature Problem

- Strong El Niños push **heat** from the ocean into the atmosphere
- Western U.S. leans warm in NOAA's Nov–Jan outlook
- Rain vs. snow can hinge on **just a degree or two**
- Warmth hits **low- and mid-elevation snowpack** hardest

Last winter: warmest on record for much of the West → precipitation fell as **rain** → **snow drought**

**Encouraging:** forecasters think a repeat of last winter's extreme warmth is unlikely.

---

# Lessons From Past El Niños in Utah

- **1982–83** — very strong El Niño; spring 1983 brought record flooding (State Street ran as a river; the Thistle landslide) and began the Great Salt Lake's rise to record levels in the mid-1980s
- **1997–98 & 2015–16** — not the snow bonanzas many expected in northern Utah

**Takeaway:** Utah's El Niño history ranges from historic to forgettable.

<!--
Use this slide to reinforce the causation vs. correlation theme. Ask: "If you only remembered 1983, what would you predict? If you only remembered 2015–16?"
-->

---

# Why This Winter Matters So Much

- Last winter **drained Utah's "water savings account"** (reservoirs, soil moisture)
- The **Great Salt Lake** sits only inches above its historic low
- State climate officials call this a **critical year** for water resources
- Positive signs: a decent monsoon left **soils wetter** going into winter, so more snowmelt can become runoff

<!--
Sources: KUER (Sept 2026), mountainluxury/Great Salt Lake context.
Dry soils "drink" snowmelt before it reaches streams and reservoirs; wetter soils help more runoff make it through.
-->

---

# What You Can Do With This Forecast

- **Expect uncertainty** — plan for a range of outcomes, not one
- **Watch the updates** — CPC ENSO discussion monthly (next: **Nov 12**); seasonal outlook (next: **Nov 19**)
- **Travel south?** Southern Utah & the Southwest have the best odds for a big winter
- **Be storm-ready** — heavy-precipitation events, flash flooding, and avalanches all become more likely where it does get wet
- **Keep conserving water** — one good winter won't erase last year's deficit

---

# Summary

1. **The forecast is solid:** a very strong — possibly record — El Niño is underway
2. **"Super" is not an official term;** it means "very strong" (≥ +2.0 °C)
3. **El Niño shifts the odds,** it doesn't guarantee outcomes
4. **The mechanism:** weakened trade winds → warm water moves east → storm track shifts south
5. **The West:** wetter South, drier North, warmer overall
6. **Utah:** right on the dividing line — southern Utah favored; northern Utah a coin flip, likely better later in winter

---

# Questions?

**Will it snow this winter?**
Almost certainly. **Will it be a big year?** The dice are loaded — but Utah is standing right where the odds are closest to even.
